### 4. Planned Structure & Section Outline (Beat Breakdown)

#### Beat 1: Real-Time Dynamic Range Switching Under Adrenalized Stress
- **Lead Tactical Rule**: *Never adapt your body to the attacker's preferred range; dictate spatial velocity to force the encounter into your asymmetric advantage.*
- **Combatives Reality**: Street violence is non-linear. The attacker does not stand still while you execute a four-part combination. They charge forward with flurries, duck beneath strikes, or suddenly backpedal to grab an improvised weapon.
- **The Engine of Dynamic Switching**: The fighter must read tactile pressure, closing velocity, and centerline displacement to switch ranges dynamically.
- **Kinetic Continuity in Real Time**: Momentum must never stop. When a strike is checked or an entry is jammed, the energy of the deflected weapon is redirected into the next joint linkage. If a kick is crowded, the kinetic energy drives an immediate forearm collar tie; if a takedown is stuffed, the sprawl momentum drives a snap-down or an immediate stand-up break.

#### Beat 2: Dictating Range vs. Reacting to Range: Controlling Spatial Velocity
- **Lead Tactical Rule**: *Control the closing velocity of the fight; never allow an opponent to dictate the distance where kinetic energy is exchanged.*
- **Combatives Reality**: Allowing an opponent to choose the range surrenders the initiative. If an attacker possesses heavy punching power, lingering in the pocket invites brain damage. If an attacker possesses collegiate wrestling skills, lingering in an upright chest-to-chest tie-up invites a high-amplitude slam.
- **Controlling Spatial Velocity**: The combative fighter proactively accelerates or arrests spatial closure ($\vec{v}_{\text{rel}} = \frac{d\vec{x}}{dt}$). Through angular footwork and aggressive level shifts, the defender either jams the distance inside the threat's kinetic levers (zero distance) or creates standoff clearance ($d > d_{\text{kick}}$), denying the threat their specialized weapons.

#### Beat 3: The Asymmetric Exit Corridor: Range Transitions as an Egress Vehicle
- **Lead Tactical Rule**: *Use range switching to create an exit, not to prolong violence; navigate the ranges to break contact and escape the hazard zone.*
- **Combatives Reality**: Civilian self-defense is defined by legal necessity and personal survival, not ring dominance. A fighter who transitions from striking to clinch to ground and back to striking without an exit strategy is simply waiting for an accomplice to intervene or a hidden blade to appear.
- **The Geometry of the Asymmetric Exit Corridor**: Every range transition must terminate in an exit vector. By angling 45 degrees outside the opponent's drive track, the defender creates a directional corridor where the opponent's momentum carries them away while the defender accelerates toward open space.

> [!NOTE]
> ### The Mechanics Behind It: Relative Spatial Velocity, Rotational Deflection Work, and the Unified Power Metric
>
> 1. **Relative Spatial Velocity & Interception Dynamics**:  
>    Closing distance between combatants is governed by the relative velocity vector:
>    $$\vec{v}_{\text{rel}} = \vec{v}_{\text{threat}} - \vec{v}_{\text{defender}}, \quad \Delta t_{\text{closure}} = \frac{d_{\text{initial}}}{\|\vec{v}_{\text{rel}}\|}$$
>    Linear backward retreat ($\vec{v}_{\text{defender}} \parallel \vec{v}_{\text{threat}}$) minimizes closing time because sprinting forward is roughly 30% faster than backpedaling ($v_{\text{fwd}} \approx 6.0\ \text{m/s}$ vs. $v_{\text{back}} \approx 4.0\ \text{m/s}$). Executing a 45-degree angular cut ($\theta = 45^\circ$) introduces lateral velocity components, forcing the attacker to decelerate to adjust their rotational inertia, expanding $\Delta t_{\text{closure}}$ by 40% to 60% and opening an asymmetric exit corridor.
>
> 2. **Kinetic Work: Linear Absorption vs. Rotational Deflection**:  
>    Arresting a charging threat of mass $m$ and entry velocity $v$ via direct linear opposition requires absorbing their total kinetic energy through negative muscular work:
>    $$W_{\text{linear}} = \Delta E_k = \frac{1}{2} m v^2$$
>    For an 85-kg assailant charging at $4.0\ \text{m/s}$, linear stoppage requires dissipating $680\ \text{J}$ of kinetic work across rigid shoulder and spine joints. In contrast, applying an angular deflection frame applies an eccentric force couple:
>    $$\vec{\tau} = \vec{r}_{\text{frame}} \times \vec{F}_{\text{threat}} = I_{\text{threat}} \vec{\alpha}$$
>    Converting linear momentum into rotational displacement requires less than $120\ \text{J}$ of defensive muscular work ($< 18\%$), deflecting the threat's momentum vector harmlessly past the defender's centerline.
>
> 3. **The Master Equation Across Ranges**:  
>    Combative power across multi-range transitions represents effective mechanical work delivered within the total action window:
>    $$P_{\text{effective}} = \frac{W_{\text{decisive}}}{\Delta t_{\text{decision}} + \Delta t_{\text{transition}} + \Delta t_{\text{execution}}}$$
>    Because human physiological execution time ($\Delta t_{\text{execution}}$) has fixed biomechanical constraints, true combative power is maximized by collapsing decision latency ($\Delta t_{\text{decision}} \to 0$) through invariant primitives and eliminating transition seam gaps ($\Delta t_{\text{transition}} \to 0$) through dual-purpose frames.
