# Chapter 07: The Ground Maneuver Lens: Angles, Flanking, and Structural Security

## 7.2 Security of Position: Never Ceding Structural Advantage for a Cheap Strike

### The Tactical Anchor: Security in Infantry and Combatives

Never sacrifice posture, balance, or defensive integrity to reach for an unvetted offensive strike; position is your survival insurance.

In land warfare, U.S. Army Field Manual 3-0 (*Operations*) and Army Doctrine Publication 3-0 establish **Security** as an immutable Principle of War. Security dictates that a commander never permits the enemy to acquire an unexpected advantage. When an infantry squad secures key terrain, no disciplined leader abandons that anchor to chase an isolated enemy scout. Pursuing a fleeting target exposes your flanks and walks your unit into an ambush.

In close-quarters combat on concrete, **Security of Position** represents the identical operational mandate. You never surrender structural integrity, balance, or geometric leverage to reach for an unvetted offensive opening. Your upright stance, aligned spine, and dynamic base of support constitute your fortress. They absorb incoming shock, shield vital organs, and keep your vision clear.

Untrained fighters routinely violate this principle. Under survival stress, amateurs equate aggression with wild flurries, lunging forward or diving at legs for an immediate knockout. They gamble away structural stability for an uncertain reward. 

Combatives demands a sterner doctrine: position precedes force. Launching a strike from an unstable base delivers minimal stopping power and buys you nothing if you lose your feet. On asphalt, maintaining your defensive anchor guarantees survival; gambling your base invites destruction.

### The Siren Song of the Cheap Strike: Why Fighters Compromise Their Base

A strike thrown from a broken base generates minimal damage while multiplying your own vulnerability.

Human psychology under threat contains a deadly bias: the urge to finish the fight overrides mechanical discipline. When an adversary steps backward or drops their hands, adrenaline screams at you to attack, luring you into the trap of the cheap strike.

A cheap strike is any offensive action that compromises your posture, leans off your center of mass, or lunges across dead space without securing an angle. In street footage, defenders repeatedly commit this error: they land a glancing blow, see the opponent retreat, and lunge forward with a wild follow-up punch.

Mechanically, pitching your torso past your lead knee to reach an opponent pushes your center of mass outside your base of support. You lose the ability to drive into the pavement; you no longer stand on an active platform, but catch yourself mid-fall.

Simultaneously, your cervical spine flexes forward, turning your skull into an extended lever arm. Because your mass accelerates forward out of equilibrium, an alert attacker merely posts a palm against your forehead, snaps your collar downward, or side-steps your rush. Your own unanchored momentum drives you face-first into their rising knee or down onto the concrete. You compromised your position for a cheap blow, handing the adversary total control.

### Convex Tactics: Asymmetric Risk-Reward on the Ground

In real-world violence, select techniques with negligible cost of failure and decisive upside; never choose high-downside gambles.

To preserve Security of Position under life-or-death pressure, evaluate combat actions through Decision Theory and asymmetric risk (von Neumann & Morgenstern, 1944; Taleb, 2012). A *convex payoff* represents an asymmetric condition where downside risks remain tightly bounded while upside rewards remain open and decisive. Conversely, a *concave payoff* represents a catastrophe trap: modest potential gains coupled with unbounded, ruinous downside.

Combat doctrine demands that you execute **Convex Tactics**. Select maneuvers that cost you almost nothing if they fail, but yield immediate structural dominance if they succeed. Reject concave gambles that offer a flashy victory at the risk of catastrophic positional failure.

Nowhere does this divergence appear more starkly than in takedown selection on concrete: **The Double Leg Failure Trap** versus **The Ankle Pick Advantage**.

Consider the classic wrestling blast double leg. On a cushioned wrestling mat, shooting a deep level change—dropping both knees to penetrate the opponent's hips—carries minimal environmental hazard. If the opponent sprawls hard, the referee halts the action or the wrestler scrambles safely across foam.

Transport that technique onto asphalt, and the risk profile turns deeply concave. Dropping bare knees directly onto pavement risks fracturing patellas before impact. 

More critically, evaluate the failure state. When a heavy assailant sprawls backward, they drive two hundred pounds directly onto your neck and spine. Your head pins beneath their chest, locking your cervical column into acute flexion. You cannot see threats, retreat, or deploy hands. Pinned under their torso, you absorb point-blank elbows, eye gouges, or front headlocks. If a second attacker approaches, you cannot escape incoming stomps. On concrete, a failed double leg yields absolute catastrophe.

Now examine the principle-level alternative: the ankle pick. 

The ankle pick exemplifies true tactical convexity. You establish an upper-body frame—a heavy collar tie or clavicle post—that fixes the attacker's posture and suppresses forward vision. From an upright stance, you lower your level through your hips while keeping your torso vertical and eyes forward. Your knees never touch asphalt. You hook the attacker's lead ankle with one hand while driving your upper frame backward to tilt their center of mass over their heel.

If the attacker pulls their leg back or your grip slips, your penalty is virtually zero. Your knees never touched concrete. Your head remains stacked over your hips, and feet retain full traction. You release the grip, retract your hand into a high defensive frame, and maintain upright posture on two feet with zero positional debt. 

That is the essence of Convex Tactics: you force the attacker to gamble their balance, while you risk nothing of your own.

### Positional Anchoring: The Three Structural Columns

Anchor your structure through three aligned columns—head over hips, hips over base, and elbows inside the ribcage.

Maintain Security of Position through **Positional Anchoring**, aligning **The Three Structural Columns**:

**Column 1: Head Over Hips (Cervical and Spinal Alignment)**  
Your skull weighs ten to twelve pounds. As long as your ears align vertically over your shoulders and hips, the axial strength of your vertebral column carries that mass effortlessly. Leaning into a punch turns your neck into a vulnerable cantilever beam, inviting snapdowns and guillotine chokes. Keep your chin tucked and cervical column vertical over your pelvis. When your head stays over your hips, an attacker cannot bend your spine.

**Column 2: Hips Over Base (Dynamic Center of Mass Management)**  
Your pelvis houses your body’s primary **Center of Mass**. To maintain stability, your hips must hover within the boundary formed by your feet—the **Polygon of Support**. Maintain an athletic base: knees flexed, weight distributed across the balls of your feet, base slightly wider than your shoulders. If hips drift outside your feet, your restoring moment collapses to zero. Keep your hips over your base to transmit incoming shock directly into the ground through ground reaction forces.

**Column 3: Elbows Inside the Ribcage (The Structural Wedge)**  
Your arms protect your command center: carotid arteries, jaw, and temples. Untrained fighters flare their elbows outward, opening wide corridors into their ribs and centerline. In positional anchoring, keep your elbows glued inside your ribcage. Forearms form rigid structural wedges pointing forward. Incoming hooks impact these skeletal wedges, directing kinetic energy into your braced torso and down into the ground.

Aligning these columns transforms your body into an integrated truss structure. You achieve **Structural Advantage**: you force the attacker to fight your entire skeletal mass and the ground beneath your boots, while they flail on isolated muscle power.

### Tactical Synthesis: Position Before Submission, Structure Before Strike

Secure your platform first; let the strike be a dividend of superior structure, not an overdraft on your balance.

Ground maneuver asserts an unbreakable hierarchy: position precedes submission, and structure precedes the strike.

Every combative action must align with the Master Equation: **Distance $\to$ Time $\to$ Power**.

Distance: Security of Position controls the spatial buffer. Refusing to overreach preserves the reactionary distance necessary to read intent cues, evade haymakers, and spot incoming weapons.

Time: An anchored base eliminates recovery latency. When an overextended fighter misses, they require three hundred to five hundred milliseconds to pull their torso back over their feet. When an anchored fighter misses or evaluates a threat, recovery latency is zero; they never lost their base.

Power: Power is a structural transfer of ground reaction force. A fighter punching from an unstable stance delivers only the isolated mass of their arm. A fighter striking from an anchored base drives their heels into asphalt, locks their core through aligned columns, and delivers the full kinetic mass of their grounded body.

Do not gamble your base for the illusion of offensive glory. Anchor your three columns, execute convex tactics, and let the adversary exhaust their momentum against your structure.
